\section{Petersen}
\label{sec:petersen}

% Cosas a usar: diam(P)=2, existe una \'unica geod\'esica entre cualesquiera par
% de v\'ertices, existe un conjunto dominante de 3 v\'ertices

\begin{teorema}
\label{teo:cotasup_petersen}
    If $P$ denotes the Petersen graph, then $c\paren{\t{2}{P}} \leq 4$.
\end{teorema}

\begin{proof}
    Recall that $\gamma(P)=3$, so that one may choose the initial configuration
    of the cops to be one in which they, individually, ocuppy a permutation of
    the $3$ vertices of a dominating set (see (a) in Figure \ref{fig:initial}). We
    claim that, in their first turn after choosing an initial configuration,
    cops $c_1$ and $c_2$ can capture $r_1$ and $r_2$ with their first and second
    tokens respectively. This can easily be seen from Figure \ref{fig:initial}
    since there are at least two colors in each vertex, so in case $c_{1,1}$ and
    $c_{2,2}$ were not at distance one from their respective targets, they would
    just exchange their colors (and the shapes within their new color if
    needed).

    Once the latter has happened, cops $3$ and $4$ move to capture $r_1$ and
    $r_2$ respectively, emphasizing that token $c_{i,j}$ always move towards
    token $r_j$. Thus, if $c_3$ had any of his tokens at distance $1$ from
    $r_1$, it would possibly relabel it to put $c_{3,1}$ in position to capture
    $r_1$ in the same turn (and the same would happen for $c_{4,2}$).
    Nevetheless, per cop, there is one vertex in the dominating set where no
    token of such cop is, meaning $c_{3,1}$ and $c_{4,2}$ might not capture
    their objectives. Despite this, since the diameter in $P$ is $2$, in the
    worst case both $c_{3,1}$ and $c_{4,2}$ end their turns at distance $1$ from
    $r_1$ and $r_2$ respectively (see (b) from Figure \ref{fig:initial}).

    Given the current situation, if $R$ passes, then both $c_{3,1}$ and $c_{4,2}$
    achieve their goal. Otherwise, assume without loss of generality that $R_1$
    has moved. This yields to $c_{4,2}$ capturing $r_2$, while $c_{1,1}$
    recaptures $r_1$ and $c_{3,1}$ follows the shortest path leading to $r_1$,
    which is unique and will leave it at distance one from its objective. At the
    moment, if $R$ wants to avoid both $c_{4,2}$ and $c_{3,1}$ to capture their
    objectives, he needs to keep moving $r_1$ indefinitely. Moreover, it has to
    so avoiding the vertices in the closed neighborhood of $c_{3,2}$, since
    entering to one of those would make $c_3$ to relabel their tokens, capturing
    $r_1$ with $c_{3,1}$ in that same turn. Notice that $R$ would have to move
    $r_1$ over an induced $C_6$ (not able to move back since $c_{3,1}$ is always
    following him). Therefore, either $c_2$ or $c_4$ could positionate their
    first token in a vertex of such cycle so that, eventually, $r_1$ could not
    move forward in the cycle (since both $c_2$ and $c_4$ are already capturing
    $r_2$), leaving it to pass, move backwards to $c_{3,1}$, or move into the
    neighborhood of $c_{3,2}$. Then, both $c_{3,1}$ and $c_{4,2}$ would be
    capturing their objectives (see Figure \ref{fig:cycle}).

    In the following steps, $c_{1,1}$ and $c_{3,1}$ will move to recapture $r_1$
    if such token were to be moved by $R$. In the same manner, $c_{2,2}$ and
    $c_{4,2}$ will recapture whenever $R$ moves $r_2$. Now we claim that it is
    possible to carry both $r_1$ and $r_2$ to be in adjacent vertices. Indeed,
    let $N(r_1(t))=\l{u_1,v,u_2}$ and $N(r_2(t))=\l{w_1,v,w_2}$, noting that the
    intersection of both sets is equal to $\l{v}$ since the diameter of Petersen
    is $2$ and, between any pair of vertices, there is a unique path of shortest
    lenght. Without loss of generality, assume the last token moved by $R$ is
    $r_1$. Hence, $c_1$ and $c_3$ exhausted their movement by recapturing as
    said at the beginning of the paragraph. Then, in an attempt to force $R$ to
    fix $r_1$ in its position or to get it near to $r_2$, $c_{2,1}$ and
    $c_{4,1}$ move their tokens to be adjacent to $u_1$ and $u_2$ respectively.
    Notice that this can be attained since, from any possible vertex in which
    they could be, one can move one to the neighborhood of $u_1$ and the other
    to the neighborhood of $u_2$ (see )

    Observe that $r_1$ cannot be moved to neither $u_1$ nor $u_2$ since it will
    get $R$ to lose in the next turn. Therefore, either $R$ passes or moves
    $r_2$. In the first case, both $c_{2,1}$ and $c_{4,1}$ get to $w_1$ and
    $w_2$ respectively to force $R$ to move $r_1$ closer to $r_2$ or to move
    $r_2$ itself. Assume without loss of generality that it moves to $w_1$,
    noting that any obstruction could be solved with a relabel of tokens,
    leading to the new $c_{2,1}$ or $c_{4,1}$ to be in the neighborhood of the
    new vertex to which $r_1$ could move (see ). Once more, let $N(w_1)=\l{u_1,
    r_2(t),y}$. Notice that one of $u_1$ and $u_2$ (without loss of generality
    $u_1$) is the new unique vertex in both the neighborhood of $r_1(t+1)$ and
    $r_2(t+1)$. Therefore, $c_{2,1}$ is freed of his duty to guard such vertex,
    fact that it will be fundamental later. Due to the same reason of the last
    paragraph, $c_{1,2}$ and $c_{3,2}$ can be positioned on the neighborhood of
    $r_2(t)$ and $y$ respectively. Then, if $R$ passes, just as before,
    $c_{1,2}$ and $c_{3,2}$ get to $r_2(t)$ and $y$ respectively, forcing $r_2$
    or $r_1$ to move.

    Notice that $R$ is able to place $r_1$ on $v$ since such vertex is not in
    the neighborhood of $r_2(t+1)$.
