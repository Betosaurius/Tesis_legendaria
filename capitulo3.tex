\begin{lema}
    \label{bound}
      If $P$ denotes the Petersen graph, then $c\paren{\t{2}{P}} > 3$.
\end{lema}
    
\begin{proof}
    It suffices to show that $R$ has an escape strategy against $3$ cops. It will
    be shown that $R$ is able to keep his tokens in non-adjacent vertices while
    $C$ is at least two turns away from capturing him, or that $R$ can return to
    the first situation were his tokens to be in adjacent vertices.

    First, notice that $C$ can ocuppy up to $6$ vertices, one per token per cop.
    Since $P$ has order $10$, $R$ may positionate his tokens so that no cop is
    capturing any of them, hence $C$ is at least $2$ turns away from capturing him
    (see Figure \ref{fig:initial0}). Moreover, since $P$ does not have $4$-cycles, in particular it does
    not contain a $K_4$, meaning that at least two of such vertices are
    non-adjacent.

    In the following turns, $R$ will pass whenever no cop is one movement away
    from capturing him. When such thing happens, notice that $R$ has $6$ possible
    moves, two of them which will result in their cops being in adjacent vertices
    since Petersen graph is $3$-regular, has diamater $2$ and there is a unique
    path of shortest length between any pair of vertices (see Figure
    \ref{fig:possible}). Suppose without loss of generality that $c_1$ is a cop
    which is one turn away from capturing $R$, implying that it has one of his
    tokens capturing a token of $R$, while the remaining one is at distance $1$
    from the other token of $R$. This implies that one of the tokens of $R$ has
    one of their possible movements ruled out. Furthermore, if another cop is two
    turns away from capturing, then the best way to guard as much vertices to
    which $R$ could move is to have a token in a vertex adjacent to one of the
    tokens of $C$, while the other is in a vertex adjacent to the remaining token
    of $R$. Both $c_2$ and $c_3$ could do this to cover $4$ of the $6$ possible
    moves of $R$. Then $R$ would just have one movement: move the captured token
    to be adjacent to the uncaptured one. Thus, $R$ survives the next turn since
    no cop is one turn away from capturing him (see (b) of Figure
    \ref{fig:possible}).

    We claim that, whatever $C$ does, it will not stop $R$ from carrying the last
    moved token to a new vertex $u$ (not the one it was before). Such vertex is at
    distance two from the current position of his other token, so proving this
    fact will imply that $R$ was able to keep his token in non-adjacent vertices
    while no cop is one turn away from capturing him. Indeed, note that $u$ cannot
    be ocuppied by a token of $C$ aside from one of $c_1$, which is no threat to
    $R$ since the remaining token of $c_1$ is at distance $2$ from the other token
    of $R$. Moreover, since $c_2$ and $c_3$ are $3$ turns away from capturing $R$
    after his move (as in (b) of Figure \ref{fig:possible}), then in $C$'s turn
    they can get themselves to be $2$ turns away from capturing $R$. Nonetheless,
    when $R$ moves his captured token $u$, it is either preserving its distance to
    $c_2$ in $F_2(P)$ or is increasing it in $1$ unit (see (c) and (d) in Figure
    \ref{fig:possible}). Therefore, $R$ gets back to the same initial situation
    where no cop is one turn away from capturing him and their tokens are in
    non-adjacent vertices, ensuring that he has a strategy to keep the going
    forever. Thus, $c\paren{\t{2}{P}}>3$.

\end{proof}
    
\begin{teorema}
    If $P$ denotes the Petersen graph, then $c\paren{\t{2}{P}}=4$.
\end{teorema}

\begin{proof}
    From Lemma \ref{bound} we already know that $c\paren{\t{2}{P}}\geq 4$. It
    suffices to show that $c\paren{\t{2}{P}}\leq 4$. Recall that $\gamma(P)=3$, so
    that one may choose the initial configuration of the cops to be one in which
    they, individually, ocuppy a permutation of the $3$ vertices of a dominating
    set (see (a) in Figure \ref{fig:initial}). We claim that, in their first turn
    after choosing an initial configuration, cops $c_1$ and $c_2$ can capture
    $r_1$ and $r_2$ with their first and second tokens respectively. This can
    easily be seen from Figure \ref{fig:initial} since there are at least two
    colors in each vertex, so in case $c_{1,1}$ and $c_{2,2}$ were not at distance
    one from their respective targets, they would just exchange their colors (and
    the shapes within their new color if needed).

    Once the latter has happened, cops $3$ and $4$ move to capture $r_1$ and
    $r_2$ respectively, emphasizing that token $c_{i,j}$ always move towards
    token $r_j$. Thus, if $c_3$ had any of his tokens at distance $1$ from
    $r_1$, it would possibly relabel it to put $c_{3,1}$ in position to capture
    $r_1$ in the same turn (and the same would happen for $c_{4,2}$).
    Nevertheless, per cop, there is one vertex in the dominating set where no
    token of such cop is, meaning $c_{3,1}$ and $c_{4,2}$ might not capture
    their objectives in the current turn. Despite this fact, since the diameter
    in $P$ is $2$, in the worst case both $c_{3,1}$ and $c_{4,2}$ end their
    turns at distance $1$ from $r_1$ and $r_2$ respectively (see (b) from Figure
    \ref{fig:initial}).

    Given the current situation, if $R$ passes, then both $c_{3,1}$ and
    $c_{4,2}$ achieve their goal. Otherwise, assume without loss of generality
    that $r_1$ has moved. This yields to $c_{4,2}$ capturing $r_2$, while
    $c_{1,1}$ recaptures $r_1$ and $c_{3,1}$ follows the shortest path leading
    to $r_1$, which is unique and will leave it at distance one from its
    objective. At the moment, if $R$ wants to avoid both $c_{4,2}$ and $c_{3,1}$
    to capture their objectives, he needs to keep moving $r_1$ indefinitely.
    Moreover, it has to do so by avoiding the vertices in the closed
    neighborhood of $c_{3,2}$, since entering to one of those would make $c_3$
    to relabel their tokens, capturing $r_1$ with $c_{3,1}$ in that same turn.
    Notice that $R$ would have to move $r_1$ on an induced copy of $C_6$ (not
    able to move back since $c_{3,1}$ is always following him). Therefore,
    either $c_2$ or $c_4$ could positionate their first token in a vertex of
    such cycle so that, eventually, $r_1$ could not move forward in the cycle,
    since both $c_2$ and $c_4$ are already capturing $r_2$. Hence, $R$ can only
    pass, move $r_1$ backwards to $c_{3,1}$, or move $r_1$ into the neighborhood
    of $c_{3,2}$. Then, both $c_{3,1}$ and $c_{4,2}$ would be capturing their
    objectives (see Figure \ref{fig:cycle}).

    In the following steps, $c_{1,1}$ and $c_{3,1}$ will move to recapture $r_1$
    if such token were to be moved by $R$. In the same manner, $c_{2,2}$ and
    $c_{4,2}$ will recapture whenever $R$ moves $r_2$. Now we claim that it is
    possible to carry both $r_1$ and $r_2$ to be in adjacent vertices. Indeed,
    let $N(r_1(t))=\l{u_1,v,u_2}$ and $N(r_2(t))=\l{w_1,v,w_2}$, noting that the
    intersection of both sets is equal to $\l{v}$ since the diameter of Petersen
    is $2$ and, between any pair of vertices, there is a unique path of shortest
    lenght. Without loss of generality, assume the last token moved by $R$ is
    $r_1$. Hence, $c_1$ and $c_3$ exhausted their movement by recapturing as
    said at the beginning of the paragraph. Then, in an attempt to force $R$ to
    fix $r_1$ in its position or to get it near to $r_2$, without loss of
    generality $c_{2,1}$ and $c_{4,1}$ move their tokens to be adjacent to $u_1$
    and $u_2$ respectively. Notice that this can be attained since, from any
    possible vertex in which they could be, $C$ can move one to the neighborhood
    of $u_1$ and the other to the neighborhood of $u_2$ (see Figure
    \ref{fig:guarding0}).

    $R$ to lose in the next turn. Therefore, either $R$ passes or moves $r_2$.
    In the first case, both $c_{2,1}$ and $c_{4,1}$ get to $u_1$ and $u_2$
    respectively to force $R$ to move $r_1$ closer to $r_2$ or to move $r_2$
    itself. Thus, in either case $R$ moves $r_2$ and we can assume without loss
    of generality that its new position is $w_1$. As mentioned before, $c_{2,2}$
    and $c_{4,2}$ would have to recapture $r_2$. If any obstruction were to
    exist, notice that it could be solved with a relabel of tokens, preserving
    the condition of $r_1$ not being able to move unless it gets near to $r_2$,
    leading to $R$ being forced to get $r_1$ and $r_2$ in adjacent vertices (see

    free tokens to cover the two vertices $r_1$ could go, while the two other cops
    use their free tokens to prevent $r_2$ to escape through the two vertices it
    could go, noting that there are only two possible vertices for each token
    since they are mutually blocking one vertex. Furthermore, one pair between
    $c_{1,1}$ with $c_{3,1}$ and $c_{2,2}$ with $c_{4,2}$ are capturing their
    targets, while the other is at distance $1$ from it. Therefore, $R$ is forced
    to pass during his turn, losing to $C$ in his turn (see (e) and (f) of Figure
    \ref{fig:guarding2}). Hence, $c\paren{\t{2}{P}}\leq 4$ and, consequently,
    $c\paren{\t{2}{P}}= 4$.
